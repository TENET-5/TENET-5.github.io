When cities fail in public

Across Canada, the same story repeats under different letterhead: procurement without competition, transit projects that double in cost, oversight that softens after the inquiry, and development money that never quite leaves city hall. This report scores municipalities from auditor-general findings, commissions of inquiry, sunshine lists, and other documents governments themselves published. It is not a map of rumour. II Patterns that cross city lines

Individual scandals make headlines. These threads show the system — the same contractors, the same lowball estimates, the same weakened watchdogs — moving from one city to the next.

1. Federal contractors, municipal tracks

Montréal · Ottawa LRT · Hamilton LRT · Calgary · Crown corporations

SNC-Lavalin (AtkinsRéalis) appears as vendor or sub-contractor across federal and municipal LRT projects. A corporate deferred prosecution agreement in 2019 covered $127.7 million in fraud on Libyan contracts. The Wilson-Raybould affair put political interference in the SNC prosecution on the public record. The municipal track work did not stop.

2. Developer money, zoning outcomes

Vancouver · Brampton · Mississauga · Calgary East Village

Development capital funds municipal campaigns; zoning and TIF decisions follow. Vancouver’s ABC raised $1.3 million despite 2018 provincial donation reform. The pattern is not coastal — it runs from Halifax to Victoria under different party brands.

3. LRT: estimate, overrun, repeat

Ottawa $2.1B · Toronto Crosstown $12.5B · Hamilton cancelled · Edmonton $3.1B · Calgary Green Line

Major Canadian LRT projects share consultants (Stantec, AECOM, WSP), contractors, and federal cost-sharing rules that reward optimistic early numbers. Ottawa’s inquiry found systemic management failure. Toronto placed its Crosstown contractor in default in 2023.

4. Police budgets rise as oversight thins

Vancouver +19% · Ottawa +9.2% · Toronto +7% · Hamilton 34% of tax levy · Thunder Bay OHRT

Police spending has outpaced other municipal lines while civilian oversight is cut or weakened. Thunder Bay’s 2018 OHRT finding of systemic racism is the clearest published warning that growth without accountability is not neutral.

5. Charbonneau’s unfinished business

Montréal · Laval · Longueuil · Mascouche

The 2015 Charbonneau Commission mapped a construction–political nexus in Québec. Laval’s kickback machine ran for two decades. Oversight was later relaxed; REM-era procurement has already drawn fresh irregularity claims.

6. The Sunshine List as baseline

Toronto · Calgary · Ottawa · Winnipeg

Executive pay in large cities has grown two to three times faster than inflation. Ontario’s Sunshine List ($100K+) no longer marks the exceptional; it marks the managerial floor.

III How the scores work

Each city carries a composite risk score from 0–100 built from documented findings (procurement breaches, audit failures, transit overruns, transparency gaps). Severity badges — Critical, High, Moderate, Monitored — follow those scores. Sources sit on each entry. We do not invent dollar “losses”; the proxy figure above is only a budget-weighted mass under review, not a court finding of theft.
